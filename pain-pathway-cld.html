01

Pain sensing — transduction at the nociceptor

Pain begins not in the brain but at the free nerve endings of primary sensory neurons — nociceptors — embedded in skin, muscle, and viscera. These endings do not have specialized end-organs; they are bare branches of the first-order neuron itself, tuned to detect stimuli intense enough to threaten tissue damage.

TRANSDUCTION

Heat, mechanical force, and irritant chemicals open TRP and PIEZO channels, depolarizing the ending into a graded generator potential.

THRESHOLD

If the generator potential is large enough, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels fire, and a true action potential is launched down the axon.

Aδ FIBERS

Thinly myelinated, ~5–30 m/s. Carry sharp, well-localized "first pain."

C FIBERS

Unmyelinated, ~0.5–2 m/s. Carry dull, aching, poorly localized "second pain."

02

The three-neuron relay: 1st, 2nd & 3rd order

Every conscious pain signal is relayed through exactly three neurons in series: a first-order neuron carrying the signal from skin to spinal cord, a second-order neuron that crosses to the opposite side and ascends, and a third-order neuron that carries it from thalamus to cortex.

03

Neurotransmitters at each synapse

Electrical conduction stops at every synapse. The signal is re-created chemically: an arriving action potential opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels in the presynaptic terminal, vesicles fuse with the membrane, and neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft to depolarize the next neuron.

Synapse 1 — dorsal horn

1st-order → 2nd-order neuron (Rexed laminae I–II)
Fires two transmitters together: fast glutamate (AMPA/NMDA) for immediate signaling, and slower substance P (NK-1) for sustained depolarization and central sensitization.

Synapse 2 — thalamus

2nd-order → 3rd-order neuron (VPL nucleus)
Glutamate remains the principal excitatory transmitter here, relaying the signal onward to cortex. Descending serotonergic and GABAergic input can gate (dampen) this synapse — the basis of the gate-control theory of pain.
04

The action potential: a traveling bright spot

Within a single axon, the action potential isn't a smooth wave of voltage — it's a self-regenerating spike that appears, peaks, and passes to the next patch of membrane. How it travels depends on whether the axon is myelinated. Fire both fibers below and compare their speed.

action potential glutamate vesicle substance P vesicle
Myelin forces the impulse to leap node-to-node (saltatory conduction), reaching the terminal roughly 10–15× faster than a bare, continuously-conducting C fiber of similar length.
